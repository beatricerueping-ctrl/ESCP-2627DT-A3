**Notes and Key insights from: one interview per group member**

**App Idea:** Today, most stores decide when to mark down fresh food by eye or by habit, which often means either food is thrown away or margin is given away too early. Our app replaces that guesswork with data. Staff log each delivery’s item, quantity, and freshness rating from 1 to 5\. The app then estimates how many days each batch has left, based on typical shelf lives adjusted for freshness and temperature. It compares the remaining stock with how fast it typically sells and suggests the smallest discount that should clear everything before it spoils, within the limits the store sets. A manager can accept, change, or reject each suggestion. Finally, the discounts appear in real time in the store’s customer app, so shoppers can see the current price and how it changes, and set alerts for favorite items. The app also compares its predictions with actual sales and waste to improve future estimates.

Interviewer: Beatrice Rueping   
Interviewee: Dr. Costa, Chief Sustainability Officer, KAUST University 

Questions & Answers Key Insights:   
Question 1\) How could the app’s results be linked to the UN Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 12 (responsible consumption and production) and SDG 2 (zero hunger), and which indicators would you recommend?

Response 1\) Design the app to measure more than sales and profit. The strongest links are to SDG 12.3.  SDG 2 should be presented as an indirect contribution unless the app can demonstrate improved access to nutritious food. Ideas of what to include: kg of food wasted/rescued, percentage sold before expiry, nutritional value of the food rescued, donations to food banks. 

Question 2\) Do you think that letting the app adjust prices automatically within store limits could reduce waste more effectively than a manager approving each change, or does it risk moving waste elsewhere, for example to consumers throwing the items away?

Response 2\) The automation should operate within limits set by the store. This could include setting minimum prices and maximum discounts while abiding to food-safety rules. The app could have features that allow the managers to override recommendations and provide explanations for the suggested price to build customer trust and prevent customers from being encouraged to wait for discounts. Potentially an integrated client survey could measure whether waste shifted from stores to households asking the customer whether it was thrown away at home and for what reason (eg. quality or quantity).

Question 3\) If a shopper could see the carbon footprint of a discounted item next to its price, would that change what they choose, and would the effect last?

Response 3\) A simple estimate such as ‘Food rescued \- approximately X kg of CO2 avoided’ could be useful as this may seem more tangible for the customer showing them their own impact. It is important to consider that it is not certain if the food would go to waste if it is not discounted as the goods may have been bought otherwise or the shopper could throw the goods away at home. Hence, if this kind of feature with an integrated statistic is used, it should be made clear that the estimated emissions are compared with the most likely alternative to avoid greenwashing. 

Question 4\) Would a sense of shared responsibility work better than individual alerts, for example a visible store-wide “food saved” total that shoppers and staff can see together?

Response 4\) It would be useful to have a public store dashboard with multiple KPI that are accessible to all stakeholders and most importantly the customers and grocery store workers as they are the ones driving the impact. Targets can be set as an interactive feature to promote engagement. Try to avoid using competitive rankings between competitor stores as the app concept might then become unattractive for the grocery stores or other perishable good sellers. 

Question 5\) Could the same data show which suppliers consistently deliver food that spoils early, and would that change sourcing decisions in a way that cuts waste across the whole chain?

Response 5\) In this case the app should analyze spoilage across the whole supply chain rather than immediately blaming suppliers. It may be possible to distinguish supplier issues from transport, storage or demand problems. However, if the batch quality, the delivery time, freshness, temperature, sales speed and even storage condition are all recorded simultaneously there may be an overlap, meaning that it may be challenging to distinguish the origin of the problem as food spoilage could arise from multiple conditions. Therefore, the data should first support constructive improvements with the suppliers and sourcing decisions should only change if problems continue despite corrective actions being taken. 

Question 6\) Food that is thrown away often breaks down in landfill and releases methane. Could the app’s impact be measured in emissions avoided, using the same kind of environmental impact assessment you apply in urban planning?

Response 6\) A life-cycle assessment can be integrated into the reporting system. Compare the intervention with the likely alternative: landfill, donation, composting or unsold disposal. Production, transportation, packaging, disposal and methane production emission comparisons can be included. 

Interview: Dimana Stoyanova

Industry: IT

Questions

1\. What is the most important feature this app should have?  
“Accurate and easy-to-understand price suggestions. If the recommendations are wrong or difficult to use, store employees will not trust the app.”

2\. What should the first version of the app include?  
“The MVP should track deliveries, record freshness, suggest discounts, and allow managers to approve or change prices. Extra features like customer alerts can come later.”

3\. How can the app be easy for employees to use?  
“It should have a simple interface with barcode scanning, quick freshness ratings, and clear instructions about which products need repricing.”

4\. Would the app need artificial intelligence?  
“Not at the beginning. It could start with basic rules and gradually use store data to make better predictions.”

5\. What could be the biggest problem when building the app?  
“Connecting it to different stores’ existing inventory and checkout systems. Every store may use different software.”

6\. How would we know if the app is successful?  
“We could measure whether it reduces food waste, increases sales before expiry, and protects the store’s profit margins.”

Interviewer: Aixin Shen  
Interviewee: Employee at a small family run bakery (Hamburg)  
Industry: Food Retail / Bakery  
Q\&A:  
Question 1\) How do you usually manage your daily inventory and keep track of unsold products?  
Since it’s a small family-run bakery, we don’t have a very complicated inventory system. We mainly check what’s left throughout the day and have a general idea of which products sell faster. A lot of it comes from experience rather than detailed data analysis.

Question 2\) How do you decide when and how much to discount products?  
We don’t really have a fixed strategy for discounts. Most decisions are made by the owner based on how much food is left and how busy the day has been. Since it’s a family business, we usually keep things quite simple and don’t spend too much time analysing prices.

Question 3\) What happens to bread and pastries that are not sold by the end of the day?  
We try not to waste food, so we use Too Good To Go to sell some of our leftover products. Some items can be kept for the next day if they’re still suitable for sale, but others need to be sold on the same day. There can still be some waste, depending on how much is left.

Question 4\) How useful is Too Good To Go for your bakery? Are there any limitations?  
It’s quite useful for a small business like ours because it’s an easy way to sell leftover food without having to organise everything ourselves. We can offer Surprise Bags at a lower price, and customers collect them near closing time.  
However, it doesn’t really help us decide how much to prepare in the morning or when we should start offering discounts earlier in the day. We still make those decisions ourselves.

Question 5\) Would an app that tracks remaining stock and suggests when and how much to discount products be useful?  
It sounds useful, but since we’re a small family business, we probably wouldn’t need anything too complicated. We don’t have a huge amount of stock, and the owner already knows the business quite well. It could help on quieter days, but only if it’s easy to use and doesn’t cost too much.

Question 6\) What would be the biggest concern about introducing this kind of app?  
Probably the extra time and cost. We’re already busy serving customers and preparing products, so we wouldn’t want to spend a lot of time entering information into an app. If it could work automatically or just give us a simple reminder when something isn’t selling well, that would be more useful.

Key Insights

1. Experience-Based Decisions: Small family-run bakeries may rely more on the owner’s experience than detailed inventory analysis  
2. Limited Pricing Strategy: Discounts may be decided informally rather than through a structured pricing system  
3. Existing Solution: Too Good To Go provides a convenient way to sell leftover food near closing time  
4. Limited Need for Complexity: Small businesses may not see enough value in a complicated inventory or pricing system  
5. Cost and Time Concerns: Affordability and ease of use could be major factors in whether a bakery adopts the app

For small family-run bakeries, our app should focus on:

* A basic overview of remaining stock  
* Simple discount suggestions before closing time  
* Minimal manual data entry  
* Low implementation cost  
* The ability to work alongside Too Good To Go rather than replacing it

Interviewer: Omar Husain

Interviewee: Former Japanese restaurant waiter, Cornell University student.

**Questions & Answers Key Insights**

**Question 1\) What are your responsibilities, and how involved are you in handling food or recording waste?**

**Response 1\)** I take orders, explain menu items, bring food out, and clear tables. I also tell the kitchen about allergies and order changes. I notice what customers leave behind, but I do not handle purchasing or decide whether an ingredient is still usable. If an order is wrong, I tell the shift manager. I do not weigh the leftovers, so I could describe patterns but could not give you a reliable amount of waste.

**Question 2\) What food do you most often see thrown away, and why? Is it customer leftovers, incorrect orders, or food that was not sold?**

**Response 2\)** From clearing tables, I would say rice and parts of larger combination meals are things I notice left over. Sometimes customers order several rolls to share and find they have ordered too much. A mistaken order can also mean a dish has to be remade. I cannot say which ingredients are wasted most in the kitchen because I do not see all of the preparation or closing checks. Plate leftovers and unused kitchen ingredients would need to be recorded separately.

**Question 3\) Can you describe a shift when there was unusually high food waste? What happened?**

**Response 3\)** An example in this scenario would be a Friday when a large party cancelled after the kitchen had started preparing for a busy evening. We still had regular customers, but fewer than expected. The kitchen stopped some additional preparation once it became clear the rush was not happening. I would not know exactly how much was discarded at closing. It shows why an app would need reservation changes and staff updates as well as previous sales; the forecast alone could miss a cancellation.

 

**Question 4\) When an ingredient or dish becomes unavailable, or there is more than expected, how does the kitchen communicate that to waiting staff?**

**Response 4\)** Usually the chef or shift manager tells us directly. We might also have a note beside the ordering terminal. During a rush, someone can miss the update and accept an order we cannot fulfil. For extra stock, the manager might ask us to recommend a particular dish, but I cannot set a discount myself. A shared availability list on the ordering screen would help. I would prefer that to checking a personal phone every few minutes while serving tables.

**Question 5\) Our app would predict surplus food and suggest actions, such as preparing less or offering a discount, with managers controlling the decisions. Where, if anywhere, could this help during your shifts?**

**Response 5\)** It could help if the manager got an early warning that we were unlikely to use a particular ingredient, then approved a dish for us to recommend. I would need to know the dish, its availability, the approved price, and any changes to ingredients or allergens. It would not solve food customers leave on their plates. Too much manual entry would also be difficult during service. The kitchen would still decide whether food was suitable to serve; a sales prediction should not make that decision.

**Question 6\) How do customers respond to specials or dishes recommended by staff? What might make customers hesitant about a discounted dish?**

**Response 6\)** Some customers ask what I recommend, so a clear description can help them choose. Others already know what they want and would not switch for a small discount. If an offer sounds like we are trying to get rid of old fish, I would expect questions about freshness. I would explain the offer honestly and ask the manager if I could not answer. I would also want to know whether the special reduced waste or simply replaced another dish the customer would have bought.
